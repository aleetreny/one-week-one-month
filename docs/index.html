Two clocks 01  Two clocks

6,666 research tasks · 318 fields

One week of work. One month of waiting.

AI is getting good at the research tasks that were already fast. The slow ones are slow because they wait.

Scroll

Scientists say AI already makes their work almost twice as fast.

Almost all of them expect discoveries to come sooner.1

So where does that speedup land?

0

tasks that go into research papers, across 318 fields, from numerical analysis to religious history.

Each point of light is one task.

Every task runs on two clocks.

The hours a team spends working on it…

…and the days it takes on the calendar, from start to finish.

The distance between the two is waiting. The higher a task floats above the line, the longer it waits.

Take the tasks that need about one week of hands-on work.

If AI is rated among the best at the task, it is done in three days.

If AI is rated among the worst, it takes thirty-three.

Medians of 663 tasks with 42–56 hours of work; best and worst fifth by AI rating.

Now line up every task by how well AI can do it.

Best on the left, worst on the right.

The waiting climbs.

It holds for tasks of any size.

A day of work becomes 1.1 days on the calendar for the tenth of tasks AI does best…

…and 7.6 days for the tenth it does worst.

The verbs give it away.

Tasks that start with fit, calculate or process wait a few hours.

Tasks that start with obtain, coordinate or negotiate wait one to three months.

What are they waiting for?

For a crystal to grow.

For patients to give samples.

For an archive, or a school board, to say yes.

Getting approval to use the UK statistics office’s secure microdata took a median of 50 working days last year, before any data arrived.2

AI is speeding up the clock that was already short.

The clock that sets the pace of a paper is the one AI does worst, and most of its time goes on waiting.

Faster science needs a shorter wait.

Approvals, access, samples, and the people on the other side of them: problems for institutions more than for models.

Notes

Data. SciNet (Alamos and Althoff 2026), the tasks that go into papers in 318 subfields, CC BY 4.0. “How well AI can do a task” is SciNet’s rating of how likely Claude Opus 5 is to complete it. Hours of work and calendar time are SciNet’s estimates for a typical instance of the task; a day of work is 24 hours of hands-on team time.

Robustness. The link holds inside each of the six scientific domains, and within subfields at equal hours of work, where one standard deviation more on the AI rating goes with 60% less waiting.

The picture. Every point of light is one of the 6,666 tasks. In the calendars the points are texture; the number of lit days is the median.
