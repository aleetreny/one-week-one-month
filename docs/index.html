Two clocks 01  Two clocks

Alejandro Treny Ortega · November 2026

The waiting is the hardest part.

AI and the slow side of science. AI is speeding up the parts of research that were already fast. The slow parts are slow because they wait.

scroll

Scientists say AI already makes their work almost twice as fast.1

So will science move twice as fast?

Not quite. To see why, we need to look at time.

0

different tasks go into writing a research paper, across 318 fields.

Fitting a model. Running a survey. Growing a crystal.

Each dot is one task.

Every task takes time in two ways.

Working time: the hours people actually spend on it.

Calendar time: the days from start to finish.

If nothing ever had to wait, every dot would sit on the line.

Most float above it. That gap is waiting.

Take two real tasks, each about 50 hours of work.

Fitting a statistical model of the climate, a task AI does well: done in 3 days.

Growing a crystal for a physics experiment, a task AI does badly: 33 days.

Same work. 14 hours of waiting against 30 days.

Now all 6,666 tasks, sorted by how good AI is at them.

AI is strongest on the left, weakest on the right. Up means more waiting.

The weaker AI is at a task, the longer that task waits.

Split them into five equal groups.

The fifth of tasks AI does best wait a median of 4 hours.

The fifth it does worst wait a month.

Across science, the tasks AI does worst hold a third of the hands-on work, but half of all the waiting.

Why? Look at what the tasks are.

Fit, calculate, process: work done at a computer. A few hours of waiting.

Obtain, recruit, coordinate, negotiate: work that depends on samples, permits and other people. One to three months.

What are they waiting for?

A crystal to grow.

Patients to sign up.

An archive, or a school board, to say yes.

In the UK, getting a project approved to use the national statistics office’s secure data took a median of 50 working days last year, before anyone saw a single number.2

So AI is speeding up the clock that was already fast.

The slow clock, waiting, is what sets the pace of science.

To make science faster, shorten the wait.

Any forecast of faster science that counts only the work AI can do will overstate it. Shrinking the wait depends less on smarter models than on labs, archives, ethics boards and the people on the other side of them.

Notes

Data. SciNet v1.10.0 (Alamos and Althoff 2026), the 6,666 tasks that go into research papers in 318 subfields, CC BY 4.0. “How good AI is at a task” is SciNet’s rating of how likely Claude Opus 5 is to complete it, from pairwise comparisons made by a language model. Working hours and calendar time are SciNet’s estimates for a typical instance of the task; waiting is calendar time minus working time.

Shares. The shares of work and waiting weight tasks by how often they are done and by the number of researchers in each field.

Robustness. The pattern holds in all six scientific domains, and within subfields at equal hands-on hours, where one standard deviation more on the AI rating goes with 60% less waiting.

The picture. Every dot is one of the 6,666 tasks. In the calendars and the verb bars the dots are texture; what counts is the number of lit days and the length of each bar.
