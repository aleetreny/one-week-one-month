Two clocks 01  Two clocks

6,666 research tasks · 318 fields

One week of work. One month of waiting.

AI is speeding up the parts of science that were already fast. The slow parts are slow because they wait.

scroll

Scientists say AI already makes their work almost twice as fast.1

So will science move twice as fast?

Not quite. To see why, we need to look at time.

0

different tasks go into writing a research paper, across 318 fields.

Fitting a model. Running a survey. Growing a crystal.

Each dot is one task.

Every task takes time in two ways.

Working time: the hours people actually spend on it.

Calendar time: the days from start to finish.

If nothing ever had to wait, every dot would sit on the line.

Most float above it. That gap is waiting.

Take tasks that need about one week of work.

When AI is good at the task, it is finished in 3 days.

When AI is bad at it, it takes 33.

Same work. Eleven times the wait.

Now all 6,666 tasks, sorted by how good AI is at them.

AI is strongest on the left, weakest on the right.

The weaker AI is at a task, the more that task waits.

Put it in days.

Where AI is strongest, a day of work takes about one day on the calendar.

Where AI is weakest, the same day of work stretches to almost eight.

Why? Look at what the tasks are.

Fit, calculate, process: work done at a computer. A few hours of waiting.

Obtain, coordinate, negotiate: work that depends on the world and on other people. One to three months.

What are they waiting for?

A crystal to grow.

Patients to give samples.

An archive, or a school board, to say yes.

Just getting approval to use the UK statistics office’s secure data took a median of 50 working days last year.2

So AI is speeding up the clock that was already fast.

The slow clock, waiting, is what sets the pace of science.

To make science faster, shorten the wait.

Approvals, access, samples and the people behind them: problems for institutions more than for AI models.

Notes

Data. SciNet (Alamos and Althoff 2026), the tasks that go into papers in 318 subfields, CC BY 4.0. “How good AI is at a task” is SciNet’s rating of how likely Claude Opus 5 is to complete it. Working hours and calendar time are SciNet’s estimates for a typical instance of the task; a day of work is 24 hours of hands-on team time.

Robustness. The link holds inside each of the six scientific domains, and within subfields at equal hours of work, where one standard deviation more on the AI rating goes with 60% less waiting.

The picture. Every dot is one of the 6,666 tasks. In the calendars the dots are texture; the number of lit days is the median.
